# Stage 1 literature review: airline pricing, flexibility, and operational risk

## Scope and verification standard

This review maps the 15 required literature areas to 22 studies whose title, authors, year, venue, and URL were checked against a publisher page, an authoritative working-paper repository, or an author-hosted published paper. The evidence search was completed on 2026-09-12. `literature/literature_master.csv` records the verification level for every study. Where only a publisher abstract and structured page snippets were accessible, the summary is limited to what those materials establish; the review does not imply full-text verification.

The search prioritized economics, operations research, transportation, and airline-industry research that bears on one of four links: demand and inventory to fares; ticket restrictions and flexibility premiums; operational reliability to fares or demand; and forecast disruption risk to operational decisions. It is a targeted primary-source map, not a systematic-review claim of exhaustive database coverage. No negative search result is treated as proof that a paper does not exist.

The project now uses BTS DB1B quarterly ticket records. DB1B can support aggregate carrier-route-quarter fare, traffic, dispersion, and realized/lagged operating-environment analyses. It cannot observe a quote timestamp, days to departure, displayed fare ladder, remaining inventory, archived forecast available when a fare was quoted, or a single flight's risk state. Accordingly, the original flight-level ex-ante hypothesis and the feasible DB1B redesign are kept separate throughout.

## Revenue management, demand learning, and fare dispersion

Gallego and van Ryzin's finite-horizon model makes the basic economic object clear: an airline should accept a sale only when its immediate margin compensates for the opportunity cost of consuming scarce capacity before the sales deadline.[1] Their network extension allows several products to share constrained resources, which is the logic behind origin-and-destination bid prices and booking-class availability.[2] These models contain stochastic, price-sensitive demand and can accommodate cancellations as a demand-process feature, but they do not treat forecast weather or failure to operate as a state that changes a customer's quoted fare.

Dana shows that yield-management dispersion can shift demand even when the seller does not know which period will be the peak.[3] That mechanism matters because a high fare is not necessarily evidence of market power or an operational-risk premium; it can be the equilibrium consequence of uncertain demand, costly capacity, and limited low-fare availability. Borenstein and Rose document how large within-carrier route dispersion is in U.S. ticket data—the expected absolute difference between two fares averaged 36% of the route mean in their sample—and link it to competition and flight density.[4] Stavins separately shows that advance-purchase and stay restrictions have measurable price effects.[5] These studies make fare composition and market structure essential controls in any DB1B dispersion analysis.

The flight-level studies come closest to the demand side of the proposed mechanism. Escobari and Gan use daily fares and seat inventories and find support for fare differences associated with selling probabilities and demand uncertainty.[6] Clark and Vincent find that some airlines respond to rivals' prices and disappearing rival inventory.[7] Escobari jointly estimates dynamic price and demand equations and reports that demand shocks have a larger positive effect on prices than anticipated sales.[8] Williams structurally separates stochastic demand shocks from intertemporal changes in willingness to pay and shows that the welfare direction of dynamic pricing depends on which force dominates.[9]

These results support a demand-to-price channel, but they also show why DB1B traffic is not an exogenous demand regressor. Ticket counts are sales at equilibrium prices, aggregated over the quarter and over heterogeneous booking dates. A regression of average fare on passengers therefore mixes demand shifts, movements along demand, capacity allocation, competition, and product composition. It may be descriptive or part of a simultaneous-equations design; it cannot be labeled the flight-level marginal demand effect without additional identification.

## Fare products, continuous pricing, and flexibility

Fiig, Le Guen, and Gauchet describe the transition from filed fares controlled through reservation booking designators to an offer-management system that calculates a price from bid price, customer choice, and contextual attributes.[10] The paper is especially useful for scope: fare families combine seats with services and restrictions; flexibility can be bundled or sold à la carte; and a destination weather forecast appears in a list of possible attributes for ancillary pricing. This is evidence that weather could enter a modern offer system, not evidence that carriers use it to reprice the base fare or that it has a measured effect.

The refund and flexibility literature is more developed around passenger uncertainty than airline reliability. Escobari and Jindapon model fully refundable and non-refundable tickets as screening contracts and find that their price gap narrows as the departure date approaches and customers resolve whether they will travel.[12] Mason's survey finds that short-haul business travelers changed arrangements in 32% of trips and argues that pay-per-change products may be more economical than a high fully flexible fare for many travelers.[13] Graham, Garrow, and Leonard observe heterogeneous cancellation and exchange behavior by traveler frequency, carrier, purchase timing, and trip direction.[14] Chen and Chen formalize a fare-lock option, showing in a dynamic model that it can raise revenue and welfare but can also raise ticket prices.[15]

The product definitions should remain separate:

- A refundable ticket transfers some risk that the passenger no longer wants or can take the trip.
- An exchangeable ticket permits itinerary changes, often with a fee and fare difference.
- A partially refundable ticket returns only a specified amount or proportion after cancellation.
- A fare lock gives the right, but not the obligation, to purchase later at a protected price.
- Airline-caused disruption protection concerns failure to deliver the scheduled service and may be governed by a different contract or regulation.

The verified literature is strong for fully refundable versus non-refundable products and for observed exchange behavior. It is thin on separately priced partial refunds and on flexibility premiums conditioned by forecast operational risk. DB1B's `TK_CARRIER_CHANGE`, `BULK_FARE`, and fare/ticket fields may help describe ticket composition, but they do not reconstruct refund and exchange rules or comparable Basic/Standard/Flex offers. A DB1B fare spread should not be called a flexibility premium without a product mapping.

## Weather, reliability, disruption costs, and recovery

The strongest fare evidence concerns delays rather than quote-time weather forecasts. Forbes exploits a change in LaGuardia operating restrictions and finds fares fell about $1.42 per additional minute of delay, with a larger price response in more competitive markets.[16] This is a product-quality channel: worsened reliability lowers willingness to pay. Britto, Dresner, and Voltes instead estimate demand and fare equations and find that lagged route delay reduces passenger demand but raises fares, consistent with higher operating costs dominating the price effect.[17] Zou and Hansen similarly find that delay costs are passed into higher fares in a broader airfare and frequency model.[18]

The apparently conflicting signs are substantively informative. Expected or persistent delay can shift customer demand left, airline marginal or schedule cost up, or both. The net fare effect need not have one sign, and realized contemporaneous weather is not interchangeable with an archived forecast available to an airline or passenger when a quote was generated. A credible design must define whether risk is an anticipated product-quality attribute, an expected cost, an inventory-capacity shock, or a proxy for route congestion.

Disruption-management research establishes the operational stakes but does not complete the pricing link. Rosenberger, Johnson, and Nemhauser optimize aircraft rerouting and cancellations after disruptions.[19] Lee, Marla, and Jacquillat combine probabilistic airport congestion forecasts with stochastic recovery decisions and report 1%-2% lower expected recovery costs than a myopic baseline in their experiments.[20] This confirms that ex-ante operating uncertainty is measurable and managerially relevant. It does not show that the information reaches the revenue-management system or changes passenger quotes.

## The close-title “operational risk” paper

Bergey and Moon's *Conditional Efficiency, Operational Risk and Electronic Ticket Pricing Strategies for the Airline Industry* must be included because its title appears to match the project.[21] Its operational-risk metrics are operational value at risk and expected shortfall computed from a Monte Carlo profit distribution. The stylized model varies demand and other inputs, allocates seats between discount and full fares, and compares pricing strategies along a risk-return frontier. It does not measure weather forecasts, delay or cancellation probability, observed fare quotes, or the interaction between a demand state and flight operating risk.

The paper therefore establishes prior work linking the words “operational risk” and airline ticket pricing, and it blocks any broad novelty claim based on that phrase alone. The project's narrower candidate contribution is different: whether a prospectively observable probability of operational disruption changes the level or demand slope of quoted fares. That distinction must be explicit in titles, abstracts, and future search terms.

## Implications for the DB1B aggregate redesign

The literature supports a feasible descriptive redesign around route-market pricing, traffic, competition, and persistent reliability, with careful timing. A defensible DB1B unit could be carrier × directional airport-pair or city-pair × quarter, with average fare and within-cell dispersion as outcomes, ticket/passenger counts treated as equilibrium quantity, and lagged historical reliability or climatological exposure treated as route attributes. Such a model would be adjacent to Borenstein and Rose, Britto et al., and Zou and Hansen rather than a test of dynamic quote pricing.

Three claims remain out of scope for DB1B alone:

1. **Quote-time forecast pricing:** no query timestamp or archived forecast can be joined to an individual offer.
2. **Flight-level demand-price transmission:** quarterly passengers do not reveal inventory, sales shocks, or the fare ladder when a quote changed.
3. **Flexible-fare option value:** DB1B does not identify comparable refund, exchange, and fare-lock terms.

If Stage 2/3 remain DB1B-only, realized weather should not be relabeled as ex-ante risk. Lagged BTS on-time performance, airport congestion, or long-run climate exposure can support an aggregate reliability or exposure association, provided the lag and information interpretation are stated. Any interaction with passenger volume should be described as an equilibrium quantity interaction unless a valid demand shifter or structural/simultaneous design is developed.
